Overmodulation in PWM Inverters

Squeezing More Voltage from Limited DC Bus Resources

In one sentence: Overmodulation is a controlled PWM technique that intentionally pushes the modulation index beyond the linear region to extract up to 15% more fundamental AC voltage from the same DC bus, enabling higher motor speeds at the cost of increased harmonic distortion and torque ripple.

🔍 What is Overmodulation?

Overmodulation occurs when the reference voltage signal in a PWM inverter exceeds the amplitude of the triangular carrier wave. In this region, PWM pulses begin to drop out, the waveform starts to clip, and the inverter gradually transitions from sinusoidal PWM toward square-wave (six-step) operation.

It's the operating zone between linear PWM (M ≤ 1.0) and six-step mode (M → ∞).

⚡ Why is it Needed?

At high motor speeds, the required stator voltage increases. Without overmodulation, the inverter would hit its voltage limit prematurely, restricting top speed and torque capability.

Key reasons:

  • Maximize voltage utilization from fixed DC bus
  • Extend motor speed range without increasing battery voltage
  • Maintain torque during field weakening
  • Reduce need for expensive high-voltage power electronics

🎯 How is it Used in Practice?

Modern motor drives don't operate in overmodulation continuously. Instead, they intelligently transition between modes based on real-time operating conditions:

  • Low to medium speed: Linear SVPWM for maximum efficiency and smooth control
  • Approaching voltage limit: Enter mild overmodulation to extend voltage headroom
  • High-speed operation: Deep overmodulation or six-step mode when voltage-saturated
  • Dynamic switching: Controller continuously adjusts M based on speed, torque demand, and DC bus voltage

This adaptive strategy balances performance, efficiency, and waveform quality across the entire operating envelope.

📐 Mathematical Foundation

Modulation Index (M)

The ratio of reference voltage amplitude to carrier wave peak.

$$ M = \frac{V_{ref\_peak}}{V_{carrier\_peak}} $$
Operating Regions:
• M ≤ 1.0: Linear SPWM
• M ≤ 1.15: Linear SVPWM
• 1.0 < M < 1.27: Overmodulation
• M → ∞: Six-step mode

Ratio of achievable fundamental RMS voltage to theoretical maximum.

$$ \eta_{DC} = \frac{V_{1,RMS}}{V_{DC} / \sqrt{2}} $$

🧠 Advanced Control Methods Using Overmodulation

Modern motor controllers don't just "turn on" overmodulation—they use sophisticated algorithms to optimize the transition and minimize adverse effects:

1. Adaptive Overmodulation Control

Technique: Real-time adjustment of modulation index based on DC bus voltage fluctuations and motor speed feedback.

Benefits:

  • Smooth transition between linear and overmodulation regions
  • Prevents abrupt torque changes during mode switching
  • Maintains maximum torque-per-ampere (MTPA) efficiency

Implementation: Lookup tables or neural network models predict optimal M for given operating conditions.

2. Field-Oriented Control (FOC) with Overmodulation Compensation

Technique: Vector control algorithms adjust d-q axis currents to compensate for voltage saturation.

Key Features:

  • Dynamic field weakening strategy
  • Voltage vector limiting within hexagon boundary
  • Feed-forward compensation for harmonic distortion

Challenge: Maintaining decoupled d-q control becomes difficult as PWM linearity degrades.

3. Model Predictive Control (MPC)

Technique: Predict future system states and optimize switching patterns over prediction horizon.

Advantages:

  • Multi-objective optimization (torque, efficiency, harmonics)
  • Constraint handling (voltage limits, current limits)
  • Seamless mode transitions

Computational Cost: Requires powerful microcontrollers or FPGAs.

🏭 Commercial Implementations: Industry Leaders

Overmodulation is not just a theoretical concept—it's a core technology in modern high-performance motor drives. Here are examples of manufacturers and platforms utilizing advanced overmodulation strategies:

Strategy: Advanced SVPWM with adaptive overmodulation for field weakening during highway cruising. Enables 15,000+ RPM operation on 350V battery system.

Key Innovation: Silicon carbide (SiC) MOSFETs allow higher switching frequencies, reducing harmonic distortion even in overmodulation.

🔋 LUCID MOTORS

Lucid Air Drive Unit

Motor Type: High-speed PMSM (up to 20,000 RPM)

🔧 SIEMENS

Industrial VFD Drives

Scale: Millions of units deployed globally in manufacturing automation.

🏆 Industry Trend: Silicon Carbide (SiC) Enabling Better Overmodulation

The shift to SiC power semiconductors is revolutionizing overmodulation implementation:

  • Higher switching frequencies (40-100 kHz): Allows finer PWM resolution even in overmodulation, reducing harmonic distortion
  • Lower switching losses: Makes high-frequency operation thermally feasible
  • Better thermal performance: Enables more aggressive voltage extraction without overheating
  • Smaller passive components: Higher frequencies reduce filter inductor/capacitor size

Result: Next-generation EVs can push deeper into overmodulation while maintaining acceptable waveform quality—extracting maximum performance from compact, efficient powertrains.

🛠️ Practical Design Considerations

When to Use Overmodulation

  • ✅ High-speed operation where voltage is limited
  • ✅ Field weakening region of PMSM drives
  • ✅ Temporary overload capability needed
  • ✅ Cost-sensitive designs avoiding high-voltage DC bus
  • ✅ Applications where some torque ripple is acceptable

When to Avoid Overmodulation

  • ❌ Precision servo applications requiring smooth torque
  • ❌ Low-noise requirements (medical, HVAC)
  • ❌ Systems with strict harmonic limits (grid-tied inverters)
  • ❌ Motors with poor thermal management
  • ❌ Safety-critical applications requiring deterministic control
